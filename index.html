§01 — About

Why computer engineering

I grew up between the United States and Korea and think comfortably in both languages. I chose Computer Engineering over Computer Science on purpose: if AI eventually writes most of the code itself, the harder and more interesting problem left is building the physical layer humans and AI systems use to talk to each other — sensors, actuators, interfaces. That's the throughline connecting a pandemic-era robot arm to a wearable biosensor built years later, and it's why I've been circling optics lately as the next place that problem shows up.

I'm currently a first-year at Michigan State University, and I'm looking to transfer into a program with deeper research infrastructure in robotics and embedded systems. Outside of coursework, I've tutored math and English, taught myself whatever a project needed that I didn't already know, and generally preferred building the thing over reading about it.

A note on how I think

The year I prepped for Korea's college entrance exam (the CSAT), I made a rule: no phone, no internet searches, during any study session. No instant answers — just textbooks and re-deriving things until a pattern clicked. One calculus problem took me over thirty minutes, and that was the point. It taught me that depth beats speed and that a good question is often worth more than a quick answer — an idea that later became the actual design brief behind Edu Pointer (see Product & Design below), and shapes how I still approach a new problem.

Predicting Shark Evolution with a Genetic Algorithm

15ecomorphotypes classified
~80page final report
3future-ocean scenarios modeled

Ocean ecosystems are changing under climate change at least as fast as land ecosystems, but they're studied far less — mostly because they're so much harder to observe directly. We picked sharks as a tractable way in: a predator that sits in food chains across almost every marine environment.

We classified sharks into 15 ecomorphotypes by habitat, fin structure, tooth shape, and diet, then wrote a genetic algorithm — diversity, heredity, selection, reproduction, crossover, mutation — that evolves a population of shark_survivor objects. Size, speed, and fertility are inherited from surviving parents with random mutation; objects die on predator collision and gain fitness from feeding.

Under abundant, fast prey, the simulation converged on smaller, slower sharks — a predator-avoidance body plan. Under scarce, slow prey, it converged on longer sharks that circle in place while feeding, similar to a whale shark's oversized mouth. We then fed in projected ocean acidification, warming, and microplastic scenarios, and the model predicted a shift toward that second, feeding-optimized body type as prey grows scarcer.

This started as a standard in-school research assignment that didn't really call for this much rigor — we just kept going.

WATSON Biomedical Engineering Club · Co-president, 12-person team · 1st place, club research festival

A Wearable That Reads Heart Rate, Muscle Signal, and Temperature

3sensor types fused
12person team, co-led
1stplace, academic festival

As co-president of WATSON, our school's biomedical engineering club, I helped build a wearable combining a PPG heart-rate sensor (AD8232), an EMG muscle-signal sensor, and a TMP36 temperature sensor — all read through Arduino and flagged as normal or abnormal on green/red LEDs and an LCD readout.

I designed and 3D-printed the arm-worn enclosure in Fusion 360 using FDM printing, splitting it into two halves so it clips onto the forearm without a fastener. We then presented the device alongside LEADERS, the school's business club, in a joint forum — not just demoing it as a working prototype, but arguing through how it could actually be positioned and sold.

The project won first place at the club's own academic festival.

Why the Night Sky Has More Couples Than Singles

101stars catalogued
43%are binary systems
38%are single-star systems

We catalogued 101 stars across 25+ winter constellations by how many stars share their system — single, binary, or higher multiples — and found single-star systems are actually the minority: about 38% single versus 43% binary and 19% triple-or-more.

Then we explained the pattern with physics instead of just leaving it as a statistic. Using Python (VPython), we built n-body gravity simulations of single, binary, and triple-star systems, tracking kinetic, potential, and mechanical energy to confirm conservation and show that triple-plus systems are dynamically unstable unless their mass ratios are almost exactly balanced — which is why they're so much rarer. We traced the pattern back to star formation itself: an early collapsing gas cloud rarely settles into one single point, which favors binaries from the start.

We presented it as a two-part school festival talk, with a fair amount of "why are there more couples than singles up there" framing that our audience found funnier than we expected.

Math education app · Core team member, product · Sept 2025–

Edu Pointer

During my CSAT gap year I studied without a phone, which meant no instant answers — just textbooks and re-derivation until a pattern clicked. That experience became the design brief for Edu Pointer: instead of revealing a full worked solution, it breaks a problem into steps, checks understanding at each one, and only offers a guiding question if you're stuck.

I used the app myself as an actual test-taker preparing for the exam, and most of its features exist because I asked for them from that seat — the engineering team built what I specified, not what I coded.
