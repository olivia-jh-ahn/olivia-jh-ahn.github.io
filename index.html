What the original project found

We classified sharks by habitat, size, diet and teeth, and checked how fin type (aplesodic vs. plesodic) and tooth shape track with prey. The simulation evolved a shark_survivor object that gains HP by eating and dies when it hits a predator.

Plentiful, fast prey produced smaller, slower sharks built to avoid predators. Scarce, slow prey produced long sharks that spin in place to feed, much like a whale shark. Abundant prey also produced more distinct species. We then projected acidification, warming and microplastic scenarios forward.

The program didn't ask for this much work. We wrote about 80 pages anyway, and it was selected as an outstanding project and presented as a poster.

More about the build

Heart rate came from a PPG sensor and an AD8232 ECG module, muscle activity from an EMG sensor, and temperature from a TMP36. The enclosure is an arm wrap modeled in Fusion 360 and FDM-printed.

The club also used Arduino devices to study emotion, comparing heart rate and EEG against sounds and images. We presented the wearable at WATSON × LEADERS, a joint forum with the school's business and economics club.

Why stars come in pairs

We surveyed 101 stars across 25+ winter constellations and found single stars are the minority. Then we simulated gravity in Python to see why.

How we explained the survey

A collapsing gas cloud rarely settles into a single point, so pairs are common from the start. Triple systems stay together only when their masses and distances are balanced very precisely. Run the triple above a few times and you'll see why most of them break apart.

We built the simulations in VPython, plotted kinetic, potential and mechanical energy to check conservation, and presented the project as a booth at the school's academic festival.
